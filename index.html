Know the Moon. Know the Bite.

From Lake Biwa's record-book bass to Lake Suwa's winter wakasagi, each of Japan's lakes keeps its own rhythm — different fish, different rules. Our guides help you pick a lake, check the moon, and time your cast.

These six lakes aren't the same kind of fishing. Lake Biwa and Lake Kasumigaura are freshwater bass water, the closest thing Japan has to a Southern U.S.-style lake fishery. Lake Saroma and Lake Ogawara are brackish coastal lagoons — Saroma's water is nearly as salty as the open sea — better known for scallops, salmon, and shijimi clams than bass. Lake Inawashiro's naturally acidic water keeps things simple (dace and crucian carp), and Lake Suwa is a small alpine lake famous for one thing: winter wakasagi from a heated boat. What ties them together is more of a shared habit than a shared certainty: checking moon phase — the major and minor feeding windows anglers have used for generations — is worth doing wherever you're headed, alongside the weather, not instead of it.

Everything You Need to Know About These Six Lakes

These six lakes were picked for how different they are, not just how big. Here's what's actually true about each one.

1. The Six Lakes at a Glance

  • Lake Biwa (Shiga): Japan's largest lake at 670 km², about 20 minutes from Kyoto Station. World-renowned for largemouth bass that have rivaled the all-tackle world record, plus native ayu and funa.
  • Lake Kasumigaura (Ibaraki): Japan's 2nd largest (220 km²), about an hour from Tokyo. Once a brackish bay, now freshwater after floodgates were built — a convenient bass lake, also known for wakasagi and catfish.
  • Lake Saroma (Hokkaido): Japan's 3rd largest (152 km²), but a coastal lagoon, not a freshwater lake — two channels connect it straight to the Sea of Okhotsk, so it's nearly as salty as the ocean. Known for scallop and oyster farming; anglers fish for salmon, herring, and flatfish, not bass.
  • Lake Ogawara (Aomori): The largest lake in Aomori, though only 11th largest nationally (63 km²). Also brackish, connected to the Pacific by the Takase River. Locally called the "Treasure Lake" for its shijimi clams, lake shrimp, and winter wakasagi.
  • Lake Inawashiro (Fukushima): Japan's 4th largest (103 km²) and one of its clearest, thanks to naturally acidic water that limits plant and fish life. Expect dace and crucian carp rather than bass, with some areas suited to lure fishing.
  • Lake Suwa (Nagano): Much smaller than the rest (13 km²), but one of Japan's best-known winter fishing spots — heated "dome boats" go out for wakasagi from autumn through winter, on a lake famous for its Omiwatari ice-ridge phenomenon.

2. Reading the Moon Before You Go

Solunar theory — the idea that fish feed hardest during two daily major periods (roughly two hours, centered on the moon passing overhead or underfoot) and two shorter minor periods (around moonrise and moonset) — has been a fixture of angling advice since the 1930s. It's on solid ground for tidal water: the moon drives real tides on Saroma and Ogawara, and a moving tide is a genuine reason fish feed harder. For the freshwater lakes on this list, there's no actual tide for the moon to create, and controlled studies haven't found a consistent effect once weather, light levels, and barometric pressure are accounted for.

That doesn't make it useless — it's a heuristic anglers have leaned on for generations, and a major period that happens to land at dawn or dusk is still a good window to be on the water for. Worth checking before you go, alongside the weather. Not a reason to skip a day the tables call "poor." 3. Rules Change From Lake to Lake

  • Biwa & Kasumigaura: largemouth bass are classified as invasive in Japan, so catch-and-release is standard practice and often the law — no license is needed to fish from shore.
  • Saroma & Ogawara: much of each lake is working water, managed by local fishery cooperatives for scallops, oysters, and salmon. Stick to public bank access and posted recreational areas.
  • Suwa: wakasagi fishing normally happens through a licensed dome-boat operator, not independent bank fishing.
  • Inawashiro: open, license required

4. Boat Rental & Local Access

Marinas around Biwa and Kasumigaura rent bass boats by the half or full day. Ogawara has lakeside rental facilities for its own fishing. Suwa's dome-boat trips include rod, bait, and a heated seat as part of the price. Saroma is more locally guided — a fishery cooperative office or lakeside shop is the fastest way to find out what's allowed and what's biting.

Pro tip: check the moon phase alongside the weather, not instead of it. A major period during a stable, overcast morning usually beats a major period in bright midday sun.
