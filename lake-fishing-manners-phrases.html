Lake Fishing Manners & Japanese Phrases

The etiquette that keeps a Japanese fishing spot pleasant, and the handful of phrases that actually help — no textbook Japanese required.

Fishing etiquette in Japan is mostly common sense, but a few specifics are worth knowing before you go — where written rules leave off, unwritten ones pick up. Below is what's actually documented about fishing manners here, a few things specific to the six lakes we cover, and a short list of Japanese phrases that solve real, practical situations: asking permission, renting gear, and being a decent neighbor to whoever's fishing next to you.

The etiquette that matters most

None of this is law — it's documented custom, the kind that determines whether the person next to you is glad you showed up.

  • Keep your distance. The common guideline is 5–10 meters from another angler. Lines tangle at closer range, and a hook flying past someone at that distance is a real, not theoretical, injury risk.
  • Greet people. A simple hello to anglers already at a spot is considered genuinely important in Japanese fishing culture — more so than small talk generally.
  • Pack out everything, including your line. Cut-off fishing line left behind is a real hazard to birds and other wildlife. Many regular anglers carry a small pouch or winder just for used line and other trash rather than leaving anything at the spot.
  • Keep quiet, especially early or late. Voices carry over water, and so do car doors and idling engines in a quiet pre-dawn parking area — a real complaint source near residential shorelines.
  • Don't light up the water at night. Shining a light across the surface when others are fishing scatters fish for everyone nearby; step back from the water to change lures or bait instead of doing it right at the edge.
  • Keep gear compact on breakwaters and paths. Spread-out tackle blocks foot traffic and gets stepped on — pack tight wherever people need to walk through.
  • Stay off private facilities. Aquaculture pens and enclosed fish-farming areas (ikesu) are someone's livelihood, not a fishing spot, even when they're not clearly marked.
  • Wear a life jacket. Standard safety advice at every official source we checked, for both bank and boat fishing.
Source

Japan Association for Construction Human Resources, fishing etiquette guide — nippon.jac-skill.or.jp

A few things specific to these six lakes

The general etiquette above applies everywhere. A few points are specific enough to these lakes that they're worth repeating here, with more detail on our rules by lake page.

  • Biwa & Kasumigaura: local practice is to not release caught bass or bluegill back into the water — keep or dispose of what you catch rather than returning it, even at the same lake.
  • Saroma & Ogawara: large parts of both lakes are working aquaculture water for scallops, oysters, and shijimi. Stay on public bank and port areas rather than near the rafts and cultivation zones.
  • Suwa: lure fishing is banned entirely on the lake and its connected rivers — don't assume the rules from a bass lake carry over here.
  • Inawashiro: night fishing is prohibited outright, not just discouraged.

Phrases for getting set up

These are the ones that solve an actual problem: whether you're allowed to fish somewhere, where to get a permit, or how to rent a rod.

ここで釣りをしてもいいですか？Koko de tsuri wo shite mo ii desu ka?

"Is it okay to fish here?" — worth asking if a spot looks informal or you're not sure it's open to the public.

遊漁券はどこで買えますか？Yugyoken wa doko de kaemasu ka?

"Where can I buy a fishing permit?" — useful at Suwa, Inawashiro, or Ogawara, where one is actually required.

立入禁止ですか？Tachiiri kinshi desu ka?

"Is this off-limits?" — a quick way to check before stepping past a rope, buoy line, or unclear boundary.

竿を借りたいですSao wo karitai desu

"I'd like to rent a rod" — the basic line at any rental counter or tackle shop.

近くに釣具屋はありますか？Chikaku ni tsuriguya wa arimasu ka?

"Is there a tackle shop nearby?" — good for a forgotten hook or a last-minute bait run.

いくらですか？Ikura desu ka?

"How much is it?" — pairs with almost everything above.

Phrases for while you're fishing

The social ones — the small talk that greeting culture actually expects.

こんにちはKonnichiwa

"Hello" — the standard daytime greeting to an angler already at the spot.

おはようございますOhayou gozaimasu

"Good morning" — genuinely the more common greeting at a dawn spot, since so many people arrive early.

何が釣れていますか？Nani ga tsurete imasu ka?

"What's biting?" — a completely normal opener with a neighbor, and often genuinely useful information.

大きいですね！Ookii desu ne!

"That's a big one!" — the natural thing to say about someone else's catch.

すみませんSumimasen

"Excuse me" / "sorry" — for getting someone's attention, or for a tangled line that was your fault.

お先に失礼しますOsaki ni shitsurei shimasu

Literally "excuse me for going ahead of you" — the polite thing to say when you're leaving a spot before the people near you.

ありがとうございますArigatou gozaimasu

"Thank you" — for advice, a tip on bait, or anything else a neighbor shares with you.

None of these require fluent Japanese to land. Saying even one of them, even imperfectly, tends to go a long way at a fishing spot where greeting people is already part of the culture.
