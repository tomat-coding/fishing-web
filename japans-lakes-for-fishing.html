The Six Lakes We Cover

What's actually swimming in each lake, when to go, and how to get there — Biwa, Kasumigaura, Saroma, Ogawara, Inawashiro, and Suwa.

These six lakes don't have much in common besides being on this list. Two are freshwater bass lakes, two are brackish coastal lagoons that barely qualify as "lakes" in the usual sense, one is a naturally acidic lake with limited species, and one is famous for exactly one thing: winter smelt from a heated boat. Below is the practical version of each — what's in the water, the best time to go, and how to actually get there.

Japan's largest lake

Lake Biwa (Shiga)

670 km² · ~20 min from Kyoto Station (Otsu) · freshwater

What's in the water

Non-native largemouth bass are the main draw — Biwa has produced fish that have rivaled the all-tackle world record, and it's the closest thing Japan has to a Southern U.S.-style bass lake. It's also home to two species found nowhere else on Earth: the Biwa trout (a landlocked salmon, Oncorhynchus masou rhodurus) and the giant Biwa catfish. Native ko-ayu — a dwarf sweetfish that spends its entire life in the lake instead of migrating to the sea — are a beloved family catch, plus crucian carp throughout.

Best season

Bass

Fishable close to year-round, but April–November is the reliable stretch. Spring (March–May) brings fewer bites but the biggest fish of the year, pre-spawn. Fall is when numbers pick up — an easy, forgiving season good for beginners and families. Winter (December–March) is quiet but rewards patience with a shot at a genuine trophy in deep water.

Ko-ayu

Roughly April through August, peaking May–July, fished with a long pole and simple sabiki rig from shore.

Getting there & rules

No license is needed for bass fishing — Biwa is legally classified as a "sea district" with no established fishing rights, so shore fishing is open to anyone. Bass boat rental is easy to find around the lake. The shallower south side and the deeper north side (avg. depth around 30m) fish quite differently, so it's worth knowing which shore you're on before you start.

Japan's 2nd largest lake

Lake Kasumigaura (Ibaraki)

220 km² · ~1 hr from Tokyo (Ueno Station) · freshwater (once a brackish bay)

What's in the water

Largemouth bass again, though this is known as a more technical, sometimes stingy lake compared to Biwa — part of why serious anglers rate it. Also channel catfish, pond smelt, crucian carp, and gobies. Ayu from the rivers feeding the lake are a well-known local grilled dish.

Best season

Bass, catfish & crucian carp

Spring and fall are the two recommended windows — spring for pre-spawn fish, fall for numbers. Some local guides point specifically to September. Summer fishing is possible but hot and humid.

Getting there & rules

Same as Biwa — no license is required to fish from shore. Shoreline fishing is popular with locals; boat rental ("leisure boats") clusters on the western side of the lake. If you visit between late July and mid-October, the historic sail-powered Hobikibune boats operate as a (non-fishing) tourist attraction worth seeing between casts.

Japan's 3rd largest lake — a coastal lagoon, not fresh water

Lake Saroma (Hokkaido)

152 km² · ~30 km from Abashiri City · brackish, nearly seawater

What's in the water

This one is genuinely different. Two narrow gaps in a 20km sandbar connect the lake directly to the Sea of Okhotsk, so the water is close to full seawater salinity. Scallop and oyster farming — run by local fishery cooperatives — is the lake's real business. Anglers fish the margins and river mouths for chum and pink salmon, herring, flatfish, and smelt. Bass are not part of the picture here.

Best season

Salmon

Follows Hokkaido's autumn run — roughly September through late November, best in the early morning.

Smelt

Continues into winter, sometimes fished through the ice once the shallows freeze.

Getting there & rules

Much of the lake is working aquaculture water, so stick to public shoreline and river-mouth areas rather than the scallop rafts. A local fishery cooperative office or lakeside shop is the best source for what's currently allowed and biting — don't assume the same open-access rules as Biwa or Kasumigaura apply here.

Largest lake in Aomori (11th largest nationally)

Lake Ogawara (Aomori)

63 km² · near Misawa, ~30–60 min from Hachinohe/Aomori by JR · brackish

What's in the water

Another brackish lake, connected to the Pacific by the Takase River. Ogawara calls itself Japan's top wakasagi (pond smelt) resource, and locally its shijimi clams and lake shrimp round out the "three treasures" people talk about. Also eel, dace, and crucian carp.

Best season

Wakasagi (ice fishing)

Roughly December through February — the lake's marquee season. The Ane-numa inlet is a well-known, easy spot: shallow, calm, and often set up with pre-drilled holes and rental gear for beginners.

Getting there & rules

Outside winter, the lake sees general summer fishing alongside swimming and kayaking, though wakasagi season is the specific reason most people make the trip. Guided smelt tours provide all equipment, which is the easiest way in if you don't already own ice-fishing gear.

Japan's 4th largest lake

Lake Inawashiro (Fukushima)

103 km² · gateway: Inawashiro Station · freshwater, naturally acidic

What's in the water

Naturally acidic water (around pH 5) keeps things simple: expect dace and crucian carp, with smelt reported in some of the less acidic areas, rather than the bass and trout found elsewhere on this list. What the lake lacks in species variety it makes up for in clarity — it's nicknamed "Tenkyo-ko," the Mirror of Heaven, for how sharply it reflects Mount Bandai on a calm day.

Best season

Dace & crucian carp

Fishing here is a summer activity, alongside swimming, kayaking, and windsurfing — best conditions run from late spring through early autumn. This isn't a winter ice-fishing lake like Ogawara or Suwa.

Getting there & rules

Boat rental is available lakeside for exploring the shoreline and coves at your own pace, and some areas are specifically set up for lure fishing.

Much smaller than the rest — famous for one thing

Lake Suwa (Nagano)

13 km² · Kamisuwa Station, ~2–2.5 hrs from Tokyo (Azusa Limited Express) · freshwater

What's in the water

Wakasagi is essentially the whole story here — fished not through a hole cut in the ice, but from inside a heated "dome boat" floating on the lake. It's an unusual, genuinely family-friendly way to fish straight through winter.

Best season

Wakasagi (dome boat)

Mid-October through the end of February, peaking November to mid-January. Catching over 100 fish in two hours isn't unusual on a good day. In especially cold years, the lake freezes solid and produces omiwatari — a rare ice-ridge phenomenon tied to local legend, though it's become less reliable as winters warm.

Getting there & rules

Dome boat operators provide all gear, so no equipment of your own is needed. Trips can be booked through lakeside inns, sightseeing boat operators, or pleasure-boat companies around the lake.
